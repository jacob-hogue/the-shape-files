Map Projections: Flattening a Curved Earth

No flat map can keep everything a globe keeps. Mercator kept compass directions, surveyors kept distances nearly true by keeping their zones small, and web maps kept buildings looking square.

History
Cartography
Why every flat map gives something up: Mercator’s 1569 chart, Wright’s arithmetic, Lambert’s trade-off, Tissot’s circles, the State Plane and UTM grids, and why Web Mercator is for looking, not measuring.

By Jacob Hogue

Published November 18, 2026

12 min read

On August 2, 2018, Google Maps told its followers that on its desktop map, “Greenland’s projection is no longer the size of Africa. Just zoom all the way out.”1 Zoomed all the way out, the map had become a globe. Zoom in to a street, though, and web maps are flat again. They draw every street on one flat map, a descendant of a chart made for sailors in 1569.2,3

In File 003, Latitude, Longitude and the Datum Problem, coordinates were angles, measured on a curved model of the Earth that a datum pins to the real one. But screens and paper are flat, and so are the grids most measurements are worked out on. A map projection is the rule that turns latitude and longitude into flat x and y.4 The flattening always costs something. Measured in a web map’s own units, a field near Meades Ranch, the Kansas survey station from File 003, comes out with about two-thirds more area than it has. Every flat map keeps some things true and lets others go, and which ones it keeps depends on what the map is for.

The station’s datasheet, from the National Geodetic Survey, shows that choice made twice. Its mark, a disk set in concrete when the station was reset, has two flat addresses, one on a Kansas state grid and one on a worldwide grid called the Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM), both in meters.5 The datasheet also says how much each grid shrinks or stretches distances at the mark. Why does one mark need two flat addresses, and why can’t either one keep distances true?

Mercator kept the directions

Sailors in the sixteenth century needed a flat chart that kept one thing true. A navigator steered one compass bearing at a time and plotted it with a ruler, so a straight line on the chart had to be a line of constant bearing. The usual charts spaced the parallels evenly and drew every degree of longitude as wide as it is at the equator. At the latitude of Kansas, such a chart drew the land about 1.29 times too wide but kept its north–south size. That threw off the angles between courses, so a line of constant bearing came out curved.6,7 Gerard Mercator, a mapmaker in Duisburg, fixed this with his world map of 1569 (Fig. 1). One of its legends explains that he “gradually increased the degrees of latitude toward each pole in proportion to the lengthening of the parallels.”6

His rule was to stretch every place north–south by the same amount it was already stretched east–west. At the latitude of Kansas both stretches are 1.29, so small shapes and the angles between courses come out true, and a straight line on the chart is a constant bearing. Areas, though, are stretched both ways, so at that latitude they come out about two-thirds too big. Only the equator is drawn at true size, and the stretch grows toward the poles.7 The same rule draws Greenland about 16 times its real size. On the map it looks about as big as Africa, which is really about 14 times larger.8

Mercator never explained how he spaced his parallels. Edward Wright, an English mathematician, published the arithmetic in 1599 and said where it came from: “the way how this should be done, I learned neither of Mercator, nor any man els.”9 He added up the stretch minute by minute of latitude, and his table of the spacing ends at 90° with the single word “Infinite.” To explain the result, he asked readers to picture a globe “inscribed into a concaue cylinder” that would “swel like a bladder,” stretching “as much in longitude as in latitude,” until it pressed flat against the cylinder (Fig. 2). It is a picture of even stretching. A common story has the chart made by shining a light through a globe onto a cylinder, but no such construction gives Mercator’s spacing.7

No flat map can keep everything

Mercator kept directions and gave up size. In 1772 the mathematician Johann Heinrich Lambert explained why every map has to give up something. He listed what a perfect map would do: keep the shapes of countries and their relative sizes, keep distances in proportion, draw the shortest routes as straight lines, and make latitude and longitude easy to read. All of it would be easy, he wrote, “if the Earth’s surface were a plane. But it is a spherical surface, and so not all conditions can be satisfied at once.”10 A triangle on the globe shows the problem. Two meridians that leave the North Pole 90° apart both meet the equator at right angles, so the three lines enclose a triangle with three right angles. No flat triangle can have that. In 1827 the German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss proved the general rule. A surface can be bent without stretching or tearing, but bending never changes its curvature, and since a flat sheet has none, nobody can lay a globe flat without stretching it.11

Tissot gave distortion a shape

Since every flat map stretches, mapmakers needed a way to measure how much, and where. Auguste Tissot gave them one in 1859, in a note to the French Academy of Sciences. Close up, he found, any map stretches the ground like a rubber sheet pulled in two directions at right angles, sometimes harder one way than the other.12 A tiny circle on the ground therefore becomes an ellipse on the map, and its size and shape show how the map stretches at that spot. The stretch in a given direction is called the scale factor. It compares a short length on the map with the map’s stated scale, so 1 means true and 1.29 means 29 percent too long.7 Fig. 3 puts the same circles, each 1,000 kilometers across on the ground, on two world maps.

On the Mercator map the circles stay round, or nearly. At any one point Mercator’s map stretches equally in every direction, so a small enough circle would stay perfectly round, and one scale factor describes the stretch there. That property is called conformal, and it keeps angles and the shapes of small features. The circles in Fig. 3 are large enough that the stretch changes across each one, so the circle on Greenland bulges toward the pole. It is also drawn more than three times as wide as a circle on the equator. On the equal-area map every circle covers the same area of paper, but away from the center the circles are squashed into ellipses. A map can have either property, but as the US Geological Survey’s manual of projections puts it, “No map can be both equal-area and conformal.”7

Small zones keep the stretch small

Many projections have lines they keep true, like Mercator’s equator, and the stretch generally grows with distance from them.7 A map of a small area can do better than one true line down its middle. Draw the whole map slightly small, and the one true line becomes two, one on each side of the middle. Between them distances come out a little short, and beyond them a little long, so the error is shared out instead of piling up toward the edges. Across a narrow zone it stays tiny. Lambert’s conformal conic, one of the projections he introduced, can be set so that two parallels of latitude are true. It can be pictured as a cone that passes through the globe along those two parallels and is then unrolled flat.13,14 It suits a state that is long from east to west and narrow from north to south, which is the shape of Kansas.

The state grids began with that kind of map. Early in 1933 George F. Syme, of North Carolina’s highway commission, asked the US Coast and Geodetic Survey for a system of plane coordinates for his state.15 Surveyors work with angles and distances, and on a flat grid they could use “only the formulas of plane surveying.”14 Oscar S. Adams, a mathematician at the Survey, designed the system on Lambert’s conic, and he wanted it on record that the idea came from engineers, “not as a result of a brain storm of some theoretical mathematician and geodesist.”15 Zones were kept narrow enough to hold errors to 1 part in 10,000, or 10 centimeters in a kilometer, with a few exceptions. Within about a year every state had a grid designed for it, and Kansas got two zones, Kansas North and Kansas South. Together the grids are now called the State Plane Coordinate System.14,16

A grid for one state was no use to an army working across continents. According to the US Geological Survey, the US Army adopted the UTM grid in 1947 for military maps of the whole world.7 UTM uses the transverse Mercator, which is Mercator’s map turned on its side, so that the line it keeps true runs north–south instead of along the equator. Lambert had invented it for the sphere, and later mathematicians fitted it to the ellipsoid. Outside the polar regions the world is cut into 60 north–south strips, or zones, and the middle of each zone is drawn 0.9996 of true size, 40 centimeters short in every kilometer. That puts two true lines about 180 kilometers either side of the middle.7 UTM’s zones are much wider than a state’s, so near the equator their edges come out as much as 1 part in 1,000 too long, a meter in every kilometer. When the state grids were redone in the 1980s for the new North American Datum of 1983 (NAD 83), the states kept their own grids rather than switch. Most American surveyors and engineers still considered UTM “unacceptable because of rapidly changing grid scale factors.”16

The Meades Ranch datasheet shows both designs at work. The Kansas North grid draws distances at the mark 4.3 centimeters short in every kilometer, measured against the ellipsoid, File 002’s Earth model. UTM zone 14 draws them 38 centimeters short.5 Both are short because the mark sits between each grid’s two true lines, and UTM’s error is about nine times larger because its zone is so much wider (Fig. 4). The pasture’s height matters as well, more than the Kansas grid does. It sits 574 meters above the ellipsoid, and bringing a kilometer measured there down to the ellipsoid shortens it by 9 centimeters, twice what the grid takes off. The datasheet prints a factor for that too, so a surveyor can turn grid distances back into distances on the ground.5

A flat address names its datum and its projection

The Kansas address has three parts: File 003’s NAD 83, unchanged; Adams’s conic, with a handful of fixed numbers; and axes in meters. The international standard for coordinates calls that combination a projected coordinate reference system.17 Because the datum stays the same and the projection’s numbers are fixed by definition rather than measured, the standard counts the projection as a coordinate conversion, which “introduces no error into the output coordinates.” The arithmetic is exact. Distances on the map are still stretched, but the stretch is part of the definition, which is why the datasheet can print it to eight decimal places. A change of datum is a coordinate transformation instead. Its parameter values “are empirically derived,” fitted to measurements, and they carry those measurements’ errors.17 So when two layers disagree, the projection’s arithmetic is rarely to blame. More often someone has tagged one of them with the wrong datum, zone, or units.

Web maps made a different choice. To make aerial images from many sources line up, they use one projection for the whole world. They chose Mercator because, as Microsoft’s mapping documentation puts it, “Square buildings should appear square, not rectangular.”3 And because the map is “used only for map display,” the documentation goes on, a simpler version will do. That version, now called Web Mercator, applies formulas meant for a sphere to latitudes and longitudes measured on the ellipsoid, so it looks like Mercator’s map but isn’t quite conformal.18 In 2007 the developers of OpenLayers, an open-source library for web maps, gave it a code of their own, 900913, which spells “Google” in digits. The first reply to the announcement pointed out that the proper hacker spelling would be 600613.19 Later that year the registry that catalogs coordinate systems declined to list Web Mercator. It relented in 2008, but its entry still calls Web Mercator “Not a recognised geodetic system.”18

In a web map’s own units, the Meades Ranch mark sits about 4,754 kilometers north of the equator. Along the ground it is about 4,343. At that latitude every kilometer on the ground measures about 1.29 kilometers in map units, the same stretch Mercator’s chart gave Kansas. Draw a 1-kilometer buffer around the mark in those units, and it covers only about 775 meters. The fix is to measure distances on a local grid such as the state’s, and areas on an equal-area map. Esri’s documentation puts it more bluntly: lengths and areas in Web Mercator “are not accurate or valid.”20

One mark, three flat addresses

None of that shows on the screen. Zoom in on the pasture at Meades Ranch, and the web map is flat again, with anything square on the ground looking square, or close to it. That is what it was built for. So the mark has three flat addresses. The Kansas grid’s is for surveyors in Kansas, UTM’s is for anyone who needs one grid around the world, and the web map’s is for anyone who just wants to look.
